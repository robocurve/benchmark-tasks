# Novelty comparison: tool-assisted spacer extraction

**Review date:** 4 November 2026  
**Category:** Impact on AI timelines  
**Status:** Current board instructions and cited primary catalog entries reviewed. The capability distinction is submitted for reviewer judgment.

## Proposed task

Use a 260 × 16 × 16 mm rigid pusher to move a separate 28 × 24 × 24 mm assembly spacer at least 40 mm through a 40 mm long, 28 mm wide, 30 mm high covered robot-assembly channel. Recover the spacer into the nest, return the pusher to its marked park area, and leave the result stable for one second. The roof and channel should physically prevent the YAM gripper from directly grasping or lifting the spacer. The intended capability is **indirect force transmission with a rigid tool to extract and recover a part from a constrained service path**.

The fixture is a simplified covered robot chassis/assembly channel and the spacer is a temporary rigid aluminium rectangular assembly part. The task represents removing that spacer to allow the next assembly operation. It must not claim calibrated press-fit, jam-force, damage, or industrial service reliability.

### Board-level assessment

The concept has a defensible distinction from direct bar insertion and direct part placement only if **the pusher is necessary and visibly causes the spacer’s displacement**. At every 20 Hz control step, the evaluator should verify aligned pusher-tip/spacer-face geometry during the required displacement. The score should also require the spacer in its nest, the pusher returned to its marked park area, and a one-second released dwell. Do not award full score from final poses alone if those poses can be reached without tool-mediated motion.

TK-S6WLTG is not an exact duplicate based on its inspected card, though it remains a nearby constrained rigid-object motion task. TK-9A278L is a closer capability-level comparison because it also measures tool-mediated pushing. Its flexible live-wire hazard-clearing outcome differs from extracting a rigid spacer through a cover and recovering both objects, but reviewers may consider the distinction too narrow. The planned hammer-claw instruction also leaves uncertainty because its setup and scoring are unknown; overlap cannot be assessed from that instruction alone.

## Conditions for a credible novelty claim

1. Make the roof, channel, spacer, pusher, nest, and marked park area visible in the setup evidence. Demonstrate that a direct gripper approach to the spacer is geometrically blocked.
2. Require at least 40 mm of spacer displacement caused by aligned pusher-tip contact with the spacer face. Record contact geometry at each 20 Hz control step; do not use a scripted attachment, teleport, or automatic insertion.
3. Score distinct measurable outcomes: spacer displacement, spacer seated in the recovery nest, pusher returned to its marked park area, and stable one-second release. Include partial credit for a meaningful intermediate outcome.
4. Include shortcut tests showing that direct grasping, knocking the spacer away, moving only the pusher, or placing the spacer without pusher contact cannot earn full score.
5. The TK-S6WLTG and TK-9A278L authenticated setups and scoring have been compared. The official RoboDojo Align Blocks procedure is documented above. Planned motor and hammer-claw entries are compared only from their instructions; their setup/scoring details remain unknown. Do not rely on fixture geometry or object names alone as the distinction.
6. Describe the physical limits honestly: simplified rigid parts and channel, no calibrated jamming or extraction force, and no claim about industrial service reliability.

## Real-world assembly and rework connection

Robotiq's Hand-E C10 manual documents serviceable spacers on a robot gripper stop plate. Section 3.4.4 (printed p. 26) describes using spacers to set the stop-plate height. Section 6.1, “Gripper cleaning” (printed p. 113), says worn spacers or the stop plate can be replaced: power down the robot and gripper, remove the stop-plate M4 screws, remove the spacer if needed, clean the mating surface, and reinstall the components with low-strength thread locker and the specified 2 Nm torque. This is concrete evidence that spacer handling is part of robot assembly and maintenance. [Robotiq Hand-E C10 Gripper Instruction Manual](https://blog.robotiq.com/hubfs/support-files/Hand-E%20C10%20User%20Manual%20UR.pdf)

The proposed task does **not** reproduce that procedure. The manual's spacer is screw-mounted on a gripper stop plate and is serviced during powered-off maintenance; this task uses a loose rectangular assembly spacer moved with a pusher through a simplified covered chassis channel, then recovered into a nest. It models no fasteners, stop plate, powered-off maintenance procedure, or Hand-E C10 hardware. The manual supports the limited motivation that robot spacer components may need assembly or replacement; it does not establish that this channel layout or extraction sequence is a standard industrial procedure.

## Verdict

Common suites include nearby drawer retrieval, object placement, tool organization, cable routing, and tool-mediated block alignment. RoboDojo Align Blocks and Robocurve TK-9A278L directly overlap at the broad capability level: both require using a held tool to push other objects. The proposed distinction is pushing a separate rigid spacer through a covered robot-assembly channel, verifying extraction, recovering the spacer, and returning the pusher. No exact match for that full sequence was identified in the cited catalogs, but reviewers may still judge the distinction too narrow.

TK-S6WLTG is the closest geometric comparator, but its inspected task slides the bar itself under a tunnel and scores its final pose; it has no separate workpiece or tool-mediated extraction. The planned hammer-claw instruction may add overlap, but its setup and scoring are unknown. Novelty remains a review judgment, and acceptance risk is material.
