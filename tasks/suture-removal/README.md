# Take out an interrupted suture: forceps lift the knot, a stitch cutter cuts flush, then draw it out toward the wound

A silicone suture practice pad lies on the table with one interrupted nylon stitch across its closed incision. The robot holds dissecting forceps in one hand and a single-use stitch cutter in the other. The forceps grip the knot and lift it a few millimetres; the cutter's hook slides in under the leg away from the knot and cuts it flush with the skin; the forceps draw the stitch out by the knot, pulling back toward the wound so its edges are not drawn apart, and lay it on a gauze square. Both instruments go back on their trays.

**Where it comes from.** Medical assistants have the core task "Help physicians examine and treat patients, handing them instruments or materials or performing such tasks as giving injections or removing sutures" (O*NET 31-9092.00, about 811,000 US jobs; USDOL/ETA, CC BY 4.0; O*NET is a trademark of USDOL/ETA). Limbs & Things, a maker of suture practice pads, teaches the removal in its suture aftercare manual (https://limbsandthings.com/media/busponzi/suture-aftercare-manual.pdf): "Grasp the knot with dissecting forceps, and then cut the suture precisely with scissors, flush with the tissue surface, so that the exposed length of suture (which may be contaminated) does not have to pass through the tissues", then "Remove the suture by pulling back towards the wound so that the wound edges are not drawn apart by a distracting force." UK and EU suture removal kits pair the forceps with a single-use stitch cutter, as here; US kits mostly pair them with small Littauer scissors, which make the same cut. Limbs & Things is named as a source only; this task is not affiliated with it.

**What this task tests.** Two instruments, one in each hand, working together on one strand at a point a millimetre or two across. The forceps are closed through their sprung arms by the gripper's squeeze, and only their jaws hold the knot, by friction: they must keep it up while the other hand works (lifted 1 mm, the stitch leaves room under it for the hook; the score asks for 2 mm; pulled 5 mm, it tears through the skin). The cutter's hook must slide in level under the lifted stitch on the leg away from the knot and press the leg onto its sharp edge with the force it takes to cut the nylon (1.0 N, held 0.2 s; a lighter press leaves it whole), with no more than 2 mm of the leg left standing above the skin, without pressing its foot 2 mm into the silicone. Then the jaws must pull the cut stitch out of the skin themselves, against its drag, back toward the wound (a pull away from it opens the incision), carry it to the gauze and let it fall there.

## Requirements

`yam-isaac-lab-arena` with Isaac Lab-Arena 0.3.1 (`source <ARENA_DIR>/yam.env`). `mechkit.py` and `demokit.py` are in this folder. Importing `mechkit` hooks `yam_arena.scene` and `yam_arena.inspect_embodiment` without changing them on disk.

## Run a trial

From this folder:

```bash
inspect-robots run --embodiment yam_arena -E task=task.py -E object_jitter=0 -E control_hz=20 \
  -E cam_width=320 -E cam_height=176 --scorer task_score --max-steps 2400 \
  --instruction "Take out the stitch: lift the knot with the forceps, cut the leg away from the knot flush with the skin with the stitch cutter, draw the stitch out toward the wound, put it on the gauze and the instruments back in their trays, and take your hands away from the pad."
```

A trial runs up to 2,400 steps (120 s of simulated time at 20 Hz). One fixed layout; no seed changes the scene.

## Scripted demo

```bash
python demokit.py task.py OUT --mode full                                   # the full-score reference, filmed
for m in light nolift away above preclosed hover drop; do python demokit.py task.py OUT_$m --no-record --no-cameras --mode $m; done   # the failure checks, each in its own session
python check_score.py                                                       # the hook's way under the stitch, the instruments' return and the scoring table, no simulator
```

The demo is a scripted reference: it reads the stitch's and the instruments' poses from the simulator, and a policy gets only the cameras. The left hand takes the forceps from their rest lightly (0.8 N, under the arms' 1.6 N spring preload, so the tips stay open) and holds them upright, the jaws closing along x. It sets the open jaws either side of the knot, squeezes them shut on it at 6 N, and lifts the knot 3 mm; while the right hand works, it keeps the knot 3 mm up by the knot's own observed height. The right hand takes the stitch cutter across its 6 mm face, the fingers on its two long edges, and leans it 50 degrees back toward -y, the hook's notch opening toward -x, so the two hands work the one stitch from either side of it. Held that way, the push on the hook turns the cutter against the length of the finger pads; pinched across its 1.2 mm thickness instead, the push turned it in the fingers from 50 to 78 degrees. The hand brings the hook down 10 mm to the +x side of the far leg, the notch's floor 1.1 mm over the skin (the foot just clear of it) and 1.2 mm toward the knot from the leg, so the leaning blade's plane crosses the leg about 2 mm up, and slides it in level across the leg, steered by the hook's own observed place: the leg goes into the notch through its mouth, meets the sharp inner edge, and the hand keeps pressing until the leg, on its spring, has taken 1.0 N for 0.2 s: then it is cut. The hook backs out through the notch's mouth before the cutter is lifted away. If the leg slips past the notch, the hook backs out 10 mm and slides in again, up to three tries. The forceps then pull the cut stitch out by the knot along its path, 13 mm up and 3.5 mm toward the wound, against its drag; out of the skin, it is a free strand in the jaws, which carry it over the gauze and open 2.5 cm above it, so it falls onto the gauze. The cutter, still leaning, is lowered by its hook's observed place to 4 mm over its tray beside the foam rest and let go, so it lies in the tray (lowered upright into the rest's 4 mm slot, a cutter held by its edges leaned in the fingers, struck the foam and was thrown); the forceps, now held shut at 6 N, go back on theirs, lowered until their tips reach the rest's floor before the hand lets go. Both hands rise and move away.

**Result** (scripted, GPU physics on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090, 20 Hz, filmed at 640 x 360): 1.0 in 1,101 steps (55.05 s of simulated time), within the 2,400-step limit. The jaws closed on the knot (6.1 mm of their 9 mm opening) and lifted it 3.5 mm at most. The hook took the far leg into its notch on the first try and pressed it onto the sharp edge: the push on the leg reached 1.03 N and held 0.2 s, and the leg was cut 0.97 mm over the skin line (the notch floor's lowest while the leg was in it); the cutter stayed at 50 degrees in the hand through the press, and its lowest corner never went below the skin (its foot rests on it). The wound's gap stayed under 0.2 mm. The jaws then pulled the cut stitch out along its whole path, carried the free strand to the gauze and opened over it; it came to rest on the gauze, 15 mm from its middle. The forceps ended standing in their rest and the cutter lying in its tray beside its rest. The film's first frame is the start state on all three cameras: no black or flat frames on any camera (1,102 frames each).

## Building it for a real trial

- **Pad:** a silicone suture practice pad (a Limbs & Things skin pad or the same kind), about 12 x 8 cm, on a 2.2 cm block, taped to the table; a fresh stitch tied by hand for each trial across the incision's middle, to the lab's standard: 3-0 monofilament nylon, simple interrupted, a square knot of 3 throws (two flat throws and a third to lock), both tails cut to 5 mm, 4 mm bites from the incision's edge, snug to the skin, the knot on the +y side, ink-marked where it leaves the skin. Replace the pad when its incision tears.
- **Instruments:** 12 cm non-toothed dissecting forceps standing tips-down in a slotted foam rest on a steel tray; a single-use stitch cutter standing hook-down in a foam rest on a second tray.
- **Gauze:** a 7.5 cm gauze square on the table, its corner over the forceps tray's.
- **Safety:** the cutter's hook is sharp; keep hands out of the station while the robot holds it.

| Value | In the simulator | How to measure it on the real setup |

## What the simulator leaves out

The stitch is not a thread. It is a rigid strand, drawn 0.6 mm thick (3-0 nylon is about 0.25 mm): a soft-body thread that thin would need far smaller physics steps. What is physical: the knot and the loop have colliders, and only the forceps' jaws move them, by friction. The knot rides a sprung lift joint normal to the skin (200 N/m), on the pad's knot-side half, which itself rides a stiff spring across the incision (3000 N/m), so a lift tugs the skin and a pull away from the wound opens the incision. Until the far leg is cut, the stitch's path through the skin is locked. Once it is cut, the strand slides out along that path (a 26 mm-radius track that carries the knot 13 mm up and 3.5 mm toward the wound) against a set drag of 0.3 N (assumed; 0.1 to 1 N for nylon drawn from a closed track), and it moves only as far as the jaws pull it: jaws that slip or open leave it where it is. Drawn out to the end of its path, the strand on the track gives way to a free rigid strand of the same knot and a 20 mm tail (1 g, heavier than real nylon so the solver holds it), put 3.5 mm above the drawn knot between the same jaws; the drawn strand keeps its colliders 0.3 s more, then goes, and the jaws settle onto the free knot. The forceps carry it to the gauze and let it go, and it falls and lies where it lands, which the scorer reads. The cutter's hook has colliders (its foot, its back with the sharp inner edge, and the blade body down to the notch): it stands on the silicone and meets the stitch's leg. Where the notch takes the leg, the leg has a collider on a spring along the cutting direction (667 N/m, so 1 N is 1.5 mm of push), and the hook must push it. What is a rule: the cut itself. It fires only when the leg sits in the hook's notch against the sharp inner edge (entered through the mouth, read in the cutter's own frame, after the hook reached under the lifted stitch from beside it, never down through it) and the push on the leg has reached 1.0 N and stayed there 0.2 s; then the leg's spring and collider go. The 1.0 N is assumed (0.5 to 2 N): no published force for cutting 3-0 nylon with a stitch cutter was found. A fresh razor severs a sharpness tester's standard polymer test thread at about 0.5 N (50 g on the BESS scale), and a single-use stitch cutter's edge is less keen than a fresh razor's. The strand parting under the edge is not simulated. The cutter's depth is read at its lowest corners. The swap from the strand on the track to the free strand is also code. The forceps' arms are a real sprung hinge closed by the gripper's squeeze; the fingers' pinch on an arm resists turning over a 4 mm patch, as a finger pad does on a 6 mm arm.
